## 1. Start With the Pitch, Not the Template

Your product pitch notes may contain a strong idea but still be hard to present: a customer problem sits beside feature lists, market claims, pricing thoughts, and unfinished questions. The decision is whether to ask Gamma to organize that material now or first decide what the audience needs to understand. I would do the second. A polished layout cannot resolve a fuzzy pitch, and an AI-generated slide can make an unsupported claim look more authoritative than it is.

For a concise 10-slide pitch, I would define one audience, one purpose, and one requested next step. For example: “Explain this product to prospective early customers and invite them to join a pilot.” That gives Gamma a useful boundary. It also gives you a standard for editing: if a slide does not help explain the product or support the ask, it may not belong.

## 2. Sort the Notes Before Generating

Gamma can help turn text into a presentation, but it cannot reliably know which rough notes are current, verified, or important unless you make that clear. Separate confirmed facts from assumptions and open questions. Mark claims that need checking, such as market size, expected results, pricing, or customer numbers. Remove private information you do not need in the deck, and avoid adding details simply to make the story sound more impressive.

A useful 10-slide sequence is:

1. Product name and one-sentence promise  
2. The customer problem  
3. Who experiences it and when  
4. What people do today and why it falls short  
5. Your product and how it works  
6. The key benefit or differentiator  
7. Evidence you can substantiate  
8. Business model or pricing approach, if decided  
9. Next steps, risks, or open questions  
10. A specific ask  

This is a starting structure, not a rule. If you have no verified evidence or settled pricing, do not fill those slides with invented certainty. Use a clearly labeled learning goal or leave the relevant point out.

## 3. Create and Refine the 10-Slide Deck

1. **Prepare a short source brief.** Put the pitch in plain text with section labels such as “Problem,” “Product,” “Evidence,” and “Open questions.” Keep important wording intact, especially product names and factual claims. If your notes contradict one another, resolve the conflict or mark it for review before generation.

2. **Open Gamma and start a presentation.** Gamma’s interface and plan features can change, so look for the option to create from pasted text or an outline. Paste the brief and ask for exactly 10 slides. State the audience, purpose, tone, and desired structure. For example: “Create a 10-slide presentation for prospective small-business customers. Explain the problem, product, evidence, and next step. Use concise headings and no unsupported statistics. Keep open questions visibly labeled.”

3. **Review the outline before choosing a visual direction.** Check that the sequence tells a coherent story and that the final slide has a clear ask. Edit, combine, or reorder sections before generating the full deck. I would check slide count here rather than assume the prompt alone enforces it.

4. **Choose a restrained theme.** Select a design that supports readability and fits the product. A clean background, consistent type, and a limited color palette usually make rough content easier to inspect. Add a logo or product image only if it is accurate and you have permission to use it.

5. **Edit one slide at a time.** Replace vague headings with specific ones, shorten crowded paragraphs, and remove repeated points. Check names, numbers, dates, and product details against your source notes. If Gamma proposes a claim you cannot verify, delete it or rewrite it as an explicit hypothesis.

6. **Check the presentation in its intended format.** Preview the deck from beginning to end, not just in the editor. Look for clipped text, tiny labels, awkward image crops, inconsistent styling, and slides that rely on context you have not provided. If you plan to present in person, check the exported or presentation view on the equipment or format you expect to use.

## 4. Keep the Deck Clear and Trustworthy

The most common trap is treating a clean design as proof that the content is ready. A generated deck can still misstate a feature, imply traction you do not have, or make a tentative price appear final. I would keep a simple verification pass for every factual claim: identify its source, confirm that it is current, and decide whether the wording accurately reflects the evidence.

Keep each slide focused on one main point. Supporting detail belongs in concise bullets or speaker notes, not in a wall of text. Use images that clarify the product or customer problem; decorative visuals can distract, and generated imagery should not be presented as a photograph of a real customer or product. Check that fonts and colors remain legible, including in a room where the screen may be viewed from a distance.

Before sharing, review Gamma’s current sharing, export, and privacy controls. Confirm who can access the deck, whether a public link is appropriate, and whether the content includes confidential or personal information. If the pitch includes regulated, contractual, financial, or technical claims, ask the relevant subject-matter or legal reviewer to check them rather than relying on slide-generation tools.

## 5. Common Questions

**Q1.** Can Gamma turn unstructured notes directly into exactly 10 slides?

**A1.** It can help draft a presentation from text or an outline, but you should specify the slide count and inspect the generated result. If the structure is unclear, organize the notes first and revise the outline before generating.

**Q2.** What should I do when Gamma adds a statistic or product claim?

**A2.** Verify it against a reliable source or your approved product information. If you cannot confirm it, remove it or label it plainly as an assumption or open question.

**Q3.** Should I export the deck as slides or share it as a link?

**A3.** Choose based on how you will present and who needs access. Check the current export options, formatting, and permissions, then preview the version your audience will actually see.
